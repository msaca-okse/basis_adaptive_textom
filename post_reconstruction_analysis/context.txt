Your colleague is pointing at something very common in metallurgy/texture papers: **reducing orientation information to a few quantitative descriptors** that can be compared, plotted, and discussed statistically. Right now you mainly show **maps and visualizations** (IPF, Rodrigues ODF, GROD maps), which are good, but reviewers often expect **summary numbers** or **distributions**.

The key is: metal/texture researchers usually want **metrics that describe orientation spread, texture strength, and grain statistics**.

Below are the things that would likely satisfy that request.

---

# 1. Grain Orientation Spread (GOS) statistics

You already compute GOS. What is missing is **statistics over grains**.

Typical things to show:

* histogram of **GOS per grain**
* mean / median GOS
* maybe **GOS vs grain size**

Example statement:

> The mean grain orientation spread is 2.8° with a standard deviation of 1.1°.

This immediately quantifies deformation.

---

# 2. Texture strength

Texture papers almost always quantify how strong the texture is.

Two very common measures:

### ODF maximum

[
\max f(g)
]

### Texture index

[
J = \int f(g)^2 dg
]

These tell you how concentrated the texture is.

You could report:

* texture index of bulk ODF
* texture index of grain ODFs

That gives a **single number describing texture strength**.

---

# 3. Orientation spread within grains

You already have GOS, but you can also report:

* max misorientation within grain
* 95th percentile misorientation
* standard deviation of orientation

This quantifies **intragranular deformation**.

---

# 4. Grain size statistics

Since you segmented grains, metal people expect:

* grain size distribution
* mean grain size
* maybe equivalent diameter.

Even if your slice is partial, the distribution is informative.

---

# 5. Fraction of grains near the fiber orientation

Since you observed a <001> fiber along the rod axis, you can quantify it.

Example:

Compute misorientation between grain orientation and ideal fiber.

Report:

* fraction within 5°
* fraction within 10°.

Example:

> 37% of grains have their <001> direction within 10° of the rod axis.

That is extremely easy to interpret.

---

# 6. ODF spread metrics (this may be what your colleague meant)

Since each pixel has an ODF, you can measure **ODF sharpness**.

Simple metrics:

### entropy

[
H = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i
]

### participation ratio

[
P = \frac{1}{\sum p_i^2}
]

Interpretation:

* small entropy → sharp orientation
* large entropy → broad orientation distribution

Map or histogram of this could be interesting.

---

# 7. PCA of the ODF (what your colleague mentioned)

PCA is not common in classical texture analysis, but it is useful for **dimensionality reduction of ODFs**.

What it would show:

* main modes of variation in orientation space
* whether the ODF variation is dominated by one component (fiber) or several clusters.

However, PCA is more of a **data science tool** than a standard metallurgical descriptor.

So if you include it, it should **not replace** the classical metrics above.

---

# 8. What texture/metals reviewers usually expect

If this were a typical JAC paper, reviewers would probably expect at least:

1. **texture strength metric**
2. **grain size distribution**
3. **GOS distribution**
4. **quantification of fiber texture**

You already have the hard part (ODFs). You just need to extract the statistics.

---

# 9. What I would add first

If you want the **most impact with minimal effort**, add three things:

### (1) histogram of GOS per grain

### (2) histogram of grain sizes

### (3) fraction of grains close to <001> fiber

These are extremely easy to compute and instantly quantitative.

---

# 10. Why single numbers matter

Visualizations are great, but reviewers often want:

* **numbers to compare with literature**
* **numbers to compare between methods**

For example:

> mean GOS = 2.9°
> texture index = 3.1
> 41% of grains within 10° of <001> fiber

Now your reconstruction produces **measurable results**, not only images.

---

# My recommendation

Before doing PCA, I would add:

1. **GOS distribution**
2. **grain size distribution**
3. **fiber alignment fraction**
4. **texture index**

Those are the metrics that metal/texture people immediately understand.
